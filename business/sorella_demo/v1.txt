SORELLA GELATO — WHAT THE KITCHEN HOLDS, AND HOW IT IS WRITTEN DOWN
A description of the business, for whoever has to build the thing that records it.


WHO THEY ARE

Sorella Gelato makes gelato in a production kitchen in Bristol and sells it from
two shops and to about thirty wholesale accounts. This description covers one
corner of it and no more: the ingredients the kitchen buys, where they sit, and
the three pieces of paper that say what came in, what went out and what was
found on the shelf. Nothing here is about flavours, recipes, batches, prices to
customers or the wholesale book. Those exist and are described elsewhere; a
person reading this one should be able to build what is in it without knowing
any of that.

Three people appear on the paper. Dan Farrugia is the head gelatiere and signs
for most deliveries. Marina Devlin owns the business and does the ordering and
the monthly count. Aoife Byrne manages the Cotham shop and fills in a waste
sheet when something is thrown away. Each of them is written on a document as a
name and nothing else, and each has a job that the business describes in a
phrase rather than a title: "head gelatiere, four days" is what Marina would
say if asked what Dan does. So a person is a name and a role, and that is all
the system needs to hold about one.


WHAT IS COUNTED

Eight things are bought and counted in this corner of the business:

  Whole milk, kitchen             counted in bags      GBP 8.90 a 10 L bag-in-box
  Whipping cream 38%              counted in cans      GBP 14.60 a 5 L jerry can
  Caster sugar (sucrose)          counted in sacks     GBP 24.50 a 25 kg sack
  Dextrose                        counted in sacks     GBP 41.00 a 25 kg sack
  Skimmed milk powder             counted in bags      GBP 62.00 a 25 kg bag
  Base 50 stabiliser              counted in cartons   GBP 276.00 a carton of 10 x 2 kg
  Sicilian pistachio paste        counted in tins      GBP 203.00 a 3.5 kg tin
  Cocoa 22/24                     counted in bags      GBP 41.00 a 5 kg bag

Each of them is one thing the kitchen uses, with a name that the staff use in
conversation and that identifies it — nobody says "item 4471", they say "the
dextrose". Each has a supplier it is bought from, a price per pack as the pack
arrives, and a unit it is counted in. Some of them carry a note about their
unit, because the unit is the single most persistent source of confusion here:
a bag of milk is ten litres and a bag of cocoa is five kilograms, and the word
is the same word. Base 50 is the worst of it. It arrives as a carton of ten
two-kilogram bags, and the kitchen counts cartons in the dry store and bags at
the bench, so the same stabiliser is two numbers in two units on the same day
and nobody converts between them out loud.

That is why a unit is a thing in its own right and not a word typed into a box.
The units that appear here are bags, cans, sacks, tins, cartons and kilos, and
each of them means something particular in this business that the word alone
does not say. Kilos appear because a bin bag is weighed rather than counted:
what is thrown away is written in kilograms whatever the thing was bought in.

Three of the eight have a level at which Marina reorders them, and she is clear
that the other five have none — not a level of zero, but nothing written down
at all. Pistachio paste is reordered at two tins, dextrose at one sack, and
whole milk at four bags. Everything else is ordered when it looks low.

Prices are per pack as at 15 June 2026 and they move. Pistachio paste has been
three different prices in two years. Whoever holds this number has to be able
to say what it was in March as well as what it is today, because the value of
what is standing in the store on a Tuesday in March is that Tuesday's price
times that Tuesday's count.


WHERE THINGS SIT

There are two places inside the business in this corner of it. The dry store is
ambient, and holds the sugar, the dextrose, the powder, the cartons of Base 50,
the cocoa and the pistachio. The walk-in chiller is kept between two and four
degrees and holds the milk and the cream. Each is a place with a name that
identifies it and a sentence saying what it is; the temperature is written the
way the business writes it, which is a range for the chiller and the word
ambient for the store.

Three suppliers deliver. Terra Nostra Ingredients brings the dry goods, the
pastes and the stabiliser, on a Tuesday, and takes two working days from an
order. Whitehall Dairy brings milk and cream three mornings a week and takes
one day. Severn Catering Supplies brings the sugar and the powder and most
general kitchen goods weekly on a Thursday, and takes three days.

A supplier is a place. That is the part of this description that surprises
people, and it is deliberate: the milk is the dairy's until the driver puts it
in the chiller, so the delivery is a movement out of Whitehall Dairy and into
the walk-in chiller, and the two ends are the same kind of thing. Terra Nostra
is in Watford and Whitehall Dairy is in Somerset; where a supplier is is
recorded as coarsely as that, because a town is all anybody ever says.

There are three more places, and they are outside the business rather than in
it. The kitchen bin is where waste goes. Staff is where a thing goes when
somebody takes it home, which is allowed and is written down. Tastings is where
a thing goes when it is opened for a trade customer or a school visit and never
comes back. None of these is a room and none of them holds anything anybody
counts; they are where stock stops being the business's, and they are places
for exactly that reason. So every place — inside, supplier, or one of these
three — has a name and a description, and only the ones inside have a
temperature and only the suppliers have a town, a lead time and a sentence
saying what they bring.


THE FIVE WAYS STOCK MOVES

Everything that happens to a thing in this corner of the business is one of
five, and each of them has a name the staff use, a sentence saying what
actually happens, and a note of what gets filled in on paper:

  Delivery in            A driver arrives, someone counts the packs against the
                         note and signs it. The duplicate delivery note is the
                         document, and it is the only one of the five that a
                         person outside the business also holds.

  Transfer between       A thing moves from the dry store to the chiller or
  stores                 back, usually because it was put in the wrong one or
                         because it has been opened. Nothing is written down.

  Thrown away            A bag, a pail or an opened tin goes in the bin,
                         because it is out of date, split, or was left out
                         overnight. It goes on the waste sheet and is weighed,
                         not counted.

  Taken by staff         Somebody takes a bag or a tin home, with permission.
                         It goes on the waste sheet too, in the pack it was in.

  Used for tastings      A tin is opened for a trade customer or a school
                         visit. Whoever opened it weighs what went and writes
                         it on the waste sheet if they remember.

The five are master data. Nobody retypes "the driver fills it in and the
customer signs it" on every delivery; it is a property of that way of moving,
written once. What kind of movement a particular movement was is a field on the
movement, pointing at one of these five rows.


THE THREE DOCUMENTS

Everything above is what the business is. What follows is what it writes.

A movement is one thing moving from one place to another, once. Whatever the
paper is, the shape underneath it is the same: what kind of movement it was,
where it came from, where it went, which thing moved, how much and in what
unit, the date on the paper, who wrote it, and whatever was written in words
rather than in a column. A delivery adds and a bin subtracts, and nothing has
to say so: the direction falls out of the two ends, because a supplier is a
place and a bin is a place and the movement names both.

Almost everything on it can be missing. Somebody weighs the bin without noting
what was in it. A tin is opened for a tasting and nobody writes who did it.
There is a bag on the floor and nobody knows which delivery it came off. The
paper is filled in by people who are also making gelato, and a system that
refuses a line because a column is empty is a system they will stop using.

The count sheet is the second document. Marina counts once a month, on a blank
ruled sheet with a date at the head and her name on it. It has no number of its
own and nothing in the business identifies one; two counts on the same day by
two people are two counts, and if they disagree that is a fact about the
business rather than a problem to be resolved before writing it down. At the
foot she writes notes in sentences.

Each line on the sheet is the third document, or rather part of it: a line
names the sheet it is on, the item as she wrote it — "Base 50, cartons",
"Dextrose" — the thing that resolves to, where she was standing when she
counted it, the number, and the unit. The paper has no column for where; she
walks through the building and it is obvious to her which store she is in, so
it has to be asked for on screen even though the sheet never asks. A line may
carry a note: one broken into, part used, or that she struck it through when
she could not find the thing. An item that has run out simply has no line, and
that is not the same as a line saying nought.


WHAT ANYBODY ACTUALLY WANTS TO KNOW

Two questions, and they are the reason for all of it.

The first is what is standing. For each thing, in each place, in each unit it
was written in: everything that arrived, everything that left, and the
difference between the two. Arrived and left are the same sum over the same
movements, grouped once by where the movement went and once by where it came
from — a pallet is an arrival at the dry store and a departure from Terra
Nostra in the same breath. The difference is what is there. It goes negative
for a supplier, and that is right: Terra Nostra's own dextrose is drawn down by
every delivery it makes to us.

Nothing is converted. A carton of Base 50 and a bag of Base 50 are two rows,
because adding them would state a conversion nobody in the business has ever
written down.

Beside the difference, two numbers that are not summed from anything: the level
at which the thing is reordered, and the price per pack. Both are facts
somebody stated on a day about the thing itself, and both are empty where
nobody stated them. The two together give the third: what is standing here, at
what it cost, which is the difference times the price. Marina asks for this in
March and in September and expects March's answer to use March's price.

The second question is what the count sheets found. The same shape — per thing,
per place, per unit — totalled off the count lines instead of off the
movements. Marina reads the two side by side and takes the difference in her
head, and where they disagree, that is the interesting part of the month. She
does not want the system to subtract them for her and present one number,
because the two are answers to different questions: one is what the paper says
should be there and the other is what she found.
